# Chromostereopsis

## Definition

Chromostereopsis is a depth illusion in which two flat colors placed side by side appear to sit at different distances from the viewer. In most viewers, red appears to float in front of blue or cyan. The eye keeps refocusing between the two depths, which makes the pair uncomfortable to look at and causes eye strain over time.[^chromostereo]

## Mechanism

- **The lens bends short wavelengths more than long ones.** This is chromatic aberration. Blue and cyan light comes into focus slightly in front of the retina, and red light slightly behind it.[^aberration]
- **The two eyes see the colors shifted by different amounts.** The pupil sits slightly off the eye's optical axis, so each eye displaces red and blue images a little differently. The brain reads that difference between the eyes as depth, the same cue it uses for real 3D objects.
- **Focus cannot settle on both colors at once.** Bringing the red into focus blurs the blue, and the reverse, so the eye keeps adjusting.

## Strongest Pairings

| Pairing | Strength | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Red on blue, blue on red** | Strongest | The wavelengths are farthest apart |
| **Red on cyan, cyan on red** | Strong | Cyan sits near the blue end of the spectrum |
| **Saturated colors on a black background** | Stronger | A dark background widens the pupil, which increases the aberration |
| **Muted or light colors** | Weak | Less pure wavelengths produce a smaller focus difference |

## Viewer Variation

The direction of the effect is not the same for everyone. Most viewers see red in front, a smaller group sees blue in front, and some see no depth at all. The direction depends on where each person's pupil sits relative to the eye's optical axis, and it can reverse with pupil size and lighting.[^chromostereo-reversal]

## Relation to Chromatic Vibration

Both effects make a color pair uncomfortable, and both involve chromatic aberration. They differ in cause and in what fixes them.

| Aspect | Chromostereopsis | Chromatic vibration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Perceived effect** | The colors sit at different depths | The shared edge shimmers |
| **Main cause** | Lens focusing red and blue at different depths | Equal lightness with opposite hues |
| **Typical pair** | Red and blue or cyan | Any saturated complements of equal lightness |
| **Fixed by a lightness difference** | Partly | Largely |
| **Fixed by desaturating one side** | Largely | Largely |

Red `#FF0000` on cyan `#00FFFF` shows both effects. The two colors differ in lightness (contrast ratio 3.19:1), which weakens the vibration, so for this exact pair the depth illusion is the more noticeable problem.

## Relation to Contrast Scores

The WCAG contrast ratio cannot detect chromostereopsis. Red on blue can pass or fail depending on the exact shades, and the depth effect is present either way. The effect depends on the light's wavelength, not on the brightness difference that WCAG measures.

## Remedies

- **Keep saturated red away from saturated blue or cyan,** especially for text and thin lines.
- **Desaturate or lighten one side,** which narrows the wavelength gap the eye has to focus across.
- **Avoid saturated red and blue on black backgrounds,** where the effect is strongest.
- **Separate the colors with a neutral border or gap.**

## Relation to hex-mirror

Inverting the hue of a red input produces cyan, so a red input in hex-mirror is likely to produce pairings that show this effect. Measuring the three vibration conditions only partly covers it, since the depth effect comes from refraction in the eye and not from the color values themselves.
