# Chromatic Vibration

## Definition

Chromatic vibration (also called color vibration or optical vibration) is the shimmering, flickering, or buzzing that appears along the edge where two colors meet. It makes the pair tiring to look at and hard to read. The effect is strongest when both colors are highly saturated, sit near opposite ends of the color wheel, and have about the same lightness.[^simcontrast]

## Conditions

All three conditions together produce the strongest vibration. Removing any one of them weakens it.

| Condition | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High saturation on both sides** | Both colors are vivid, with little gray mixed in | Neon green, hot pink |
| **Near-complementary hues** | The hues sit about 180° apart on the color wheel | Red and cyan, orange and blue |
| **Similar lightness** | Neither color is clearly brighter than the other | Red `#FF0000` and teal `#008E8E` |

## Mechanism

- **Opponent channels receive conflicting signals.** The retina compares colors in opposing pairs (red against green, blue against yellow). Complementary hues push those channels as far apart as they go.
- **The luminance channel sees no edge.** When the two colors have equal lightness, the brightness signal is flat across the boundary. The visual system reads edges mainly by brightness, so it cannot settle where the boundary is.
- **Chromatic aberration adds colored fringing.** The eye's lens focuses each wavelength at a slightly different depth, so a saturated edge picks up fringes the brain cannot fully correct.[^aberration]
- **Simultaneous contrast pushes the colors further apart.** Each color shifts its neighbor's appearance toward that neighbor's own complement, so the two colors seem to push against each other.[^albers]

## Worked Example

Red against cyan is a complementary pair, but pure cyan is much brighter than pure red. Darkening the cyan until both colors have the same brightness turns a mild clash into strong vibration.

| Pair | Relative luminance | WCAG contrast ratio | Vibration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `#FF0000` on `#00FFFF` | 0.213 vs 0.787 | 3.19:1 | Moderate, the lightness difference gives the eye an edge |
| `#FF0000` on `#008E8E` | 0.213 vs 0.213 | 1.00:1 | Strong, no lightness edge remains |

## Relation to Contrast Scores

The WCAG contrast ratio measures only the brightness difference between two colors and ignores hue. A pair that vibrates usually scores badly on it, since equal lightness gives a ratio near 1:1. A low score, however, does not show that a pair vibrates. Two dull grays of equal lightness also score 1:1 without any shimmer. Detecting vibration needs separate measures of saturation, hue opposition, and lightness difference, as proposed in [oklab-and-chromatic-vibration.md](../oklab-and-chromatic-vibration.md).

## Remedies

- **Separate the lightness.** Make one color clearly lighter or darker, enough to pass a WCAG threshold.
- **Desaturate one side.** Mixing gray into one color is enough on its own to stop the vibration.
- **Add a neutral border.** A thin white, black, or gray outline between the colors gives the eye a brightness edge.
- **Keep vivid complements apart.** Use them for large blocks that do not touch, and keep them out of text-on-background pairings.

## Relation to hex-mirror

The mirror variants that keep lightness unchanged (`mirror_Hsl`, `mirror_HSl`, `mirror_hSl`) are the ones at risk, because they can produce an opposite hue at the same lightness. The variants that invert lightness (`mirror_hsL`, `mirror_HsL`, `mirror_hSL`, `mirror_HSL`) create the brightness edge that prevents vibration. See [color-contrast-and-legibility.md](../color-contrast-and-legibility.md).
